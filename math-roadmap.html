2x + 3 = 11 Stage I Foundations · Year 7–10 · Age ~12–15

Get arithmetic and algebra into your hands, not just your head

Everything later collapses if this stage is rushed. Fluency here means you don't have to think about it.

Every subject, fully expanded

1. Arithmetic Mastery
  • Fractions — addition & subtraction with unlike denominators
  • Fractions — multiplication, division, and mixed numbers
  • Converting between fractions, decimals, and percentages
  • Decimals — place value, all four operations, rounding, significant figures
  • Percentages — percentage of a quantity, percentage increase/decrease, reverse percentage problems
  • Ratio & proportion — simplifying ratios, dividing a quantity in a given ratio
  • Direct and inverse proportion problems
  • Order of operations (BODMAS / PEMDAS)
  • Estimation and mental-math fluency, without a calculator
2. Pre-Algebra
  • Understanding variables and algebraic expressions
  • Evaluating expressions by substitution
  • Simplifying expressions — combining like terms
  • Solving one-step and two-step linear equations
  • Solving and graphing simple inequalities on a number line
  • Translating word problems into equations
3. Algebra I
  • Multi-step linear equations in one variable
  • Systems of two linear equations — solving by substitution
  • Systems of two linear equations — solving by elimination
  • Graphing linear equations — slope, x- and y-intercepts
  • Factoring — greatest common factor
  • Factoring — difference of squares
  • Factoring — quadratic trinomials
  • Solving quadratics by factoring
  • Solving quadratics by completing the square
  • Solving quadratics with the quadratic formula
  • Exponent rules — product, quotient, power rules, zero and negative exponents
  • Simplifying radicals and square roots
  • Functions — domain, range, function notation, evaluating f(x)
  • Polynomials — addition, subtraction, multiplication, basic long division
4. Geometry
  • Euclid's five postulates and axiomatic thinking
  • Angles — complementary, supplementary, vertical, angles on a line and at a point
  • Parallel lines and transversals — corresponding and alternate angles
  • Triangle congruence — SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS
  • Triangle similarity — AA, SAS, SSS similarity criteria
  • Circle theorems — angle at the center, angle in a semicircle, cyclic quadrilaterals, tangent properties
  • Area and perimeter of polygons and circles
  • Volume and surface area — prisms, cylinders, cones, spheres
  • Coordinate geometry — distance formula, midpoint formula, equation of a line
  • Writing your first two-column and paragraph proofs
5. Basic Trigonometry
  • Right-triangle ratios — sine, cosine, tangent (SOH-CAH-TOA)
  • Solving for missing sides and missing angles
  • Angles of elevation and depression
  • First look at the unit circle and radian measure
6. Number Theory
  • Prime numbers and prime factorization
  • Greatest common divisor and least common multiple, via the Euclidean algorithm
  • Modular arithmetic basics — clock arithmetic, remainders
  • Divisibility rules and simple divisibility proofs
7. Problem-Solving / Competition Math
  • Working through AMC 8–style problems
  • The pigeonhole principle
  • Basic combinatorics puzzles — counting arrangements
  • Logic puzzles and simple invariants

You can solve a system of equations without hesitation, prove two triangles congruent from the axioms, and explain — in your own words — why √2 can't be a fraction.

∫₀¹ x² dx = ⅓ Stage II Pre-University · Age ~15–18

Meet calculus, and write your first real proofs

This stage quietly decides who continues in math. Proof-writing is the actual gate — not calculus.

Every subject, fully expanded

1. Algebra II
  • Polynomial functions — end behavior, roots, the Factor Theorem, the Remainder Theorem
  • Rational functions — asymptotes, simplifying, solving rational equations
  • Exponential functions — growth & decay, e as a base
  • Logarithmic functions — properties of logs, solving log equations
  • Sequences — arithmetic and geometric
  • Series — sigma notation, sum formulas, infinite geometric series
  • Complex numbers — i, arithmetic with complex numbers, the complex plane
  • The binomial theorem and Pascal's triangle
2. Precalculus
  • Trigonometric identities — Pythagorean, sum/difference, double-angle
  • Solving trigonometric equations
  • Conic sections — parabola, ellipse, hyperbola equations
  • Polar coordinates and converting between polar and Cartesian
  • Vectors in 2D — addition, dot product, magnitude
3. Calculus (AB/BC-equivalent)
  • Limits — formal definition, evaluating algebraically, limits at infinity
  • Continuity and the Intermediate Value Theorem
  • Derivatives — definition as a limit
  • Derivative rules — power, product, quotient, chain rule
  • Applications — optimization problems, related rates, curve sketching
  • Integrals — antiderivatives, definite and indefinite integrals
  • The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
  • Integration techniques — substitution, basic integration by parts
  • First look at differential equations — separable equations
4. Statistics & Probability
  • Descriptive statistics — mean, median, mode, standard deviation, variance
  • Combinatorics — permutations, combinations, the multiplication principle
  • Probability rules — independence, conditional probability, an introduction to Bayes' theorem
  • Random variables — discrete vs. continuous
  • Common distributions — binomial, normal distribution
5. Introduction to Mathematical Proof
  • Propositional logic — AND, OR, NOT, implication, truth tables
  • Basic set theory — unions, intersections, subsets, Venn diagrams
  • Direct proof technique
  • Proof by contrapositive
  • Proof by contradiction
  • Mathematical induction — weak and strong

det(A − λI) = 0 Stage III Undergraduate Core · Age ~18–21

Where math stops being computation and becomes argument

A genuine undergraduate math degree's core. Real Analysis is the biggest filter in the whole path.

Every subject, fully expanded

1. Multivariable Calculus
  • Partial derivatives and the gradient
  • Multiple integrals — double and triple integrals
  • Change of variables and the Jacobian
  • Vector fields, line integrals, surface integrals
  • Green's Theorem
  • Stokes' Theorem
  • The Divergence Theorem
2. Linear Algebra
  • Vector spaces and subspaces
  • Linear independence, basis, dimension
  • Linear transformations and their matrix representations
  • Determinants and their properties
  • Eigenvalues and eigenvectors
  • Diagonalization
  • Inner product spaces, orthogonality, the Gram-Schmidt process
  • The Spectral Theorem
3. Real Analysis
  • Construction and properties of the real numbers, the completeness axiom
  • Sequences — convergence, Cauchy sequences
  • Series — convergence tests
  • Limits and continuity, done rigorously with ε–δ
  • Differentiability and the Mean Value Theorem
  • Riemann integration
  • Sequences and series of functions — pointwise vs. uniform convergence
  • Metric spaces — open/closed sets, compactness, connectedness
4. Abstract Algebra
  • Groups — definitions, examples, subgroups
  • Cyclic groups, symmetric groups
  • Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem
  • Normal subgroups and quotient groups
  • Homomorphisms and isomorphisms
  • Rings and fields — definitions and examples
  • Polynomial rings
  • Introduction to Galois theory
5. Ordinary Differential Equations
  • First-order ODEs — separable, linear, exact equations
  • Second-order linear ODEs — homogeneous and non-homogeneous
  • Systems of ODEs
  • Laplace transforms
6. Probability Theory
  • Axiomatic probability — the Kolmogorov axioms
  • Random variables, expectation, variance
  • Joint distributions
  • The Law of Large Numbers
  • The Central Limit Theorem
7. Discrete Mathematics
  • Propositional and predicate logic
  • Advanced counting, an introduction to generating functions
  • Graph theory — trees, connectivity, coloring, Euler and Hamiltonian paths
  • Introduction to algorithms and computational complexity

Pick a direction, and start reading actual papers

No single correct track from here. Pick one based on what pulled you in during Stage III, go deep.

Track A — Pure Mathematics

Complex Analysis
  • Holomorphic functions and the Cauchy-Riemann equations
  • Cauchy's Integral Theorem and Formula
  • Power series, Laurent series
  • Residue theory and contour integration
Topology
  • Point-set topology — open sets, compactness, connectedness
  • Continuous functions and homeomorphisms
  • Algebraic topology basics — the fundamental group
  • Covering spaces
Number Theory
  • Congruences and modular arithmetic in depth
  • Quadratic reciprocity
  • Diophantine equations
  • Introduction to algebraic number theory

Track B — Applied Mathematics

Numerical Analysis
  • Root-finding methods — bisection, Newton's method
  • Interpolation and approximation
  • Numerical integration
  • Error analysis and stability
Optimization Theory
  • Linear programming and the simplex method
  • Convex sets and convex functions
  • Lagrange multipliers and KKT conditions
Partial Differential Equations
  • The heat equation
  • The wave equation
  • Laplace's equation
  • Separation of variables, Fourier series methods

Track C — AI / ML-Oriented

Convex Optimization
  • Convex sets and convex functions
  • Duality theory
  • Gradient descent and its variants
Information Theory
  • Entropy and mutual information
  • Channel capacity
  • Source coding basics
Statistical Learning Theory
  • Bias-variance tradeoff
  • VC dimension
  • Generalization bounds
Stochastic Processes
  • Markov chains
  • Martingales

Alongside the coursework

  • Start reading real papers on arXiv in your chosen area — slowly, one section at a time

Nobody has solved your problem yet — that's the whole point

Every earlier stage had a known answer in the back of the book. This one doesn't.

Every step, fully expanded

The research process
  • Find an advisor and a genuinely open problem, small enough to be tractable in 2–4 years
  • Do a deep literature review in that narrow sub-area, not the whole field
  • Attempt original proofs or results — expect months of dead ends per real insight
  • Write results up formally in LaTeX, following your field's journal conventions
  • Present at seminars, take feedback seriously, revise
  • Submit to a conference or journal, go through peer review, revise again
  • Continue as a postdoc or faculty member, and mentor the next student on this same path

The milestone here

Your first accepted paper, or a successful thesis defense. From Year 7 to this point is typically a decade or more — that's normal, not slow.

How to actually study math, every day

The stages above tell you what to learn. This is how the learning happens — the same five habits at every stage.

  1. Solve problems by hand, daily. Watching a video explain a proof is not the same skill as producing one yourself.
  2. Re-derive results instead of memorizing them. If you can rebuild the quadratic formula from scratch, you understand it in a way memorization never gives you.
  3. Teach it to someone else. If you can't explain a proof in plain language, you've followed it, not understood it.
  4. Keep a problem journal. Write down what you couldn't solve, and return to it in two or three weeks without looking at your old attempt.
  5. Join a community. Math Stack Exchange, r/math, a local olympiad club — isolation is the easiest way to stall out.

Where people actually get stuck

Not hypothetical mistakes — the specific, repeated reasons students stop progressing.

  1. Treating proofs as optional. Most textbook problems are computation, not proof. Both are needed.
  2. Learning only from videos. Understanding a video isn't the same as reproducing the argument on a blank page.
  3. Rushing Real Analysis. The biggest gatekeeper course in the whole path — slow down deliberately here.
  4. Comparing your pace to other people's. A decade from Year 7 to a first paper is the normal timeline.
  5. Reading textbooks passively. Math is built by doing the exercises, including the ones you'd rather skip.
